    Differential equations are in important tool for modeling phenomena in the biological, physical, and social sciences. The purpose of this activity is to gain experience constructing elementary differential equations, to become familiar with basic differential equations terminology, and to learn certain graphical solution techniques.
    🔗
    Consider the following situations. For each situation, construct a differential equation or an initial value problem that mathematically describes the situation.

    Carbon-14 is a radioactive isotope that decays in such a way that the instantaneous rate of decay of the amount of Carbon-14 is directly proportional to the amount of Carbon-14 present. Let \(A\) denote the amount of Carbon-14 present after \(t\) years, and let \(k\) denote your constant of proportionality.

    3.

    he population growth model \(\displaystyle \frac{dP}{dt}=kP\) from the previous two examples is sometimes refered to as the exponential or Malthusian model of population growth, named after the British political economist Thomas Robert Malthus (1766-1834). A different, slightly more complicated model that factors in limited resources or an “environmental carrying capacity” is the \underbar logistic model developed by Pierre-Francois Verhulst (1804-1840). It involves two parameters \(k\) and \(N\text{,}\) where \(k\) denotes the usual proportionality constant and where \(N\) represents the maximum possible population amount. In this model, the instantaneous rate of change of the population is directly proportional to the \underbar product of two quantities: the population itself at that instant and the fraction of the remaining available population amount. Again, let \(P\) denote the amount of e.coli present after \(t\) hours. Assume that \(P(0)=\frac{N}{4}\text{.}\) Next to your initial value problem sketch on appropriately labeled axes what you think a plausible graph of the solution curve might look like.

    4.

    Newton’s Law of Cooling is a famous example of a differential equation model governing how an object’s temperature changes when it is introduced into a hotter or cooler environment. In this model the rate of change of the temperature of the object is directly proportional to the difference between the object’s temperature and the temperature of the surrounding environment. Suppose I bring a hot, 90 degree Celsius cup of coffee into my 20 degree Celsius MTH 304 classroom. Let \(C\) denote the temperature of my cup of coffee after \(t\) minutes, and assume that the constant of proportionality is given by \(k=-.15\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 4.

    A differential equation is an equation involving the derivative of some unknown function. The function itself is a rule involves an independent (input) and dependent (variable) variable that obeys the function definition. Sometimes for the sake of notational simplicity we “supress” the independent variable when we write the differential equation. For example, we can write the pregnant wife differential equation as
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dA}{dt}=2.5-kA \mbox{ or as }\frac{dA(t)}{dt}=2.5-kA(t). \end{equation*}
    Both equations mean the same thing; in each equation \(t\) represents the independent variable, \(A\) the dependent variable, and \(k\) a parameter. The form on the left, which I may sometimes call the “supressed form,” is easier to write, whereas the form on the right is good to keep in mind when we wish to think about the behavior of the dependent variable \(A\) at a particular \(t\) value. The next problem demonstrates this idea by showing how we can use the differential equation to determine useful information about the solution function without solving the differential equation itself.
    🔗

    Definition 5.

    The order of a differential equation is the highest-order derivative that the equation contains. In the first chapter we will only be concerned only with first-order differential equations. Such equations can always be written in the form \(\displaystyle \frac{dy}{dt}=f(t,y)\text{,}\) where \(f\) is a function of two variables. (Remember MTH 203?!) For example, in the differential equation \(\displaystyle \frac{dy}{dt}=y+t\text{,}\) we see that \(f(t,y)=y+t\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 6.

    For a first order differential equation written in “supressed form” it can sometimes be the case \(f\) is a function of the dependent variable alone. In this case we say that the differential equation is autonomous. For example, \(\displaystyle \frac{dy}{dt}=2y+1\) and \(\displaystyle \frac{dA}{dt}=2.5-kA\) are both autonomous since ( \(k\) is just a parameter).
    🔗

    Definition 7.

    If the function \(f\) can be written in the form \(f(t,y)=m(t)y+b(t)\text{,}\) we say that the differential equation is linear. For example, \(\displaystyle \frac{dy}{dt}=y+t\) is a first-order, linear (nonautonomous) differential equation.v

    Differential equations models that are developed in real life frequently are quite complicated and cannot be solved explicitly using algebraic methods. Instead one must resort to other means to draw conclusions about the solutions. There are a variety of techniques we will use over the course of the semester, many of them graphical in nature. Because we will discuss a variety of graphical approaches, it is imperative that when constructing graphs, you \underbar always label your axes appropriately.
    🔗
    For autonomous differential equations, which can be written in the form \(\displaystyle \frac{dy}{dt}=f(y)\text{,}\) one can construct a curve known as the “rate” versus “solution curve” plot. This graph is nothing more than a plot of the function \(f(y)\text{,}\) i.e. a plot of the solution curve \(y\) on the horizonal axes and the rate or derivative \(\frac{dy}{dt}=f(y)\) on the vertical axes.
    🔗

    Suppose that an initial value is given by \(y(0)=1\text{.}\) What does this graph tell us about the solution \(y(t)\) as \(t\) increases? Let’s think about this question carefully.
    • When \(y=1\) on the rate graph above, the derivative \(\frac{dy}{dt}=y'(t)\) is positive, implying the solution function \(y(t)\) is initially increasing from \(y=1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(y(t)\) is increasing from 1, yet less than 2, the rate graph tells us that \(y'(t)\) remains positive and decreasing. This implies that the solution function \(y(t)\) is increasing and concave down.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The closer \(y(t)\) becomes to \(y=2\text{,}\) the closer \(y'(t)\) becomes to zero. In essence, \(y=2\) is a horizontal asymptote.
      🔗
      🔗
    In summary, starting from the point \((0,1)\text{,}\) a solution curve would increase asymptotically in a concave down manner to \(y=2\text{.}\) “What if \(y(0)=2\) instead,” you say? In this case the solution would be a constant solution, i.e. \(y(t)=2\) for \(t \geq 0\text{.}\) We call \(y=2\) an equilibrium solution of the differential equation. (See p. 5 of our textbook)
