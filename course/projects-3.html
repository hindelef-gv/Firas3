    Worksheet The SIR Model of an Epidemic

    H1N1 flu, often called “swine flu”, caused a worldwide pandemic in 2009. The outbreak began in Mexico early in the year, and eventually the Mexican government closed many public and private facilities in Mexico City in an attempt to restrict the spread of the disease. Nevertheless, the virus spread worldwide. Against the advice of public health officials, some summer camps in the U.S. went so far as to use drugs such as Tamiflu in a prophylactic fashion. More typically, we were encouraged to wash our hands frequently, cough into our sleeves, and stay home during exams. The pandemic seemed to peak in November of 2009, and by spring of 2010 the number of cases was in rapid decline. The World Health Organization announced the end of the pandemic in August of 2010.

    Section Modeling and Epidemic

    The spread of a contagious disease through a population involves intricate interactions from the level of populations down to the level of individual cells and viruses. However, it is still possible to learn interesting and useful information from relatively simple models. A classical model, introduced by Kermack and McKendrick in 1927
     4 
    See “A contribution to the mathematical theory of epidemics” byW. O. Kermack and A. G. McKendrick, Proceedings Royal Society of London A 115, 1927, pp. 700--721.
    is called the SIR model. In this model, a population is divided into three groups---the susceptible individuals, the infected individuals, and the recovered individuals. In this model \(S(t)\) denotes the fraction of the population that can catch the disease at time \(t\text{,}\) \(I (t)\) denotes the fraction of the population that has the disease and can spread it to the susceptible, and \(R(t)\) denotes the fraction of the population that has recovered from the disease and cannot catch it again. This model is appropriate for the spread of a flu epidemic since once a person has had a particular strain of flu, their immune system prevents them from catching that strain again. Since flu spreads fairly quickly, we can assume that time is measured in days. While most people recover from the flu fairly easily, there is a low mortality rate. Those who do not survive are included in \(R(t)\text{.}\) We assume everyone in the population is either susceptible, infected, or recovered, that is,
    \begin{equation*} S(t)+I(t)+R(t)=1 \end{equation*}
    for all \(t\text{.}\) In addition, we assume that the disease spreads relatively quickly, so it is reasonable to assume that the only change in the size of these groups is due to the disease. To set up the model, we make some more specific assumptions. First, we assume that the rate that susceptible people and infected people interact is proportional to both the number of susceptible and the number of infected, that is, proportional to the product of \(S(t)\) and \(I (t)\text{.}\) Some fraction of these interactions lead to a susceptible becoming infected. We also assume that the infected individuals recover at a rate that is proportional to the number of infected. Based on these assumptions, our model is
    \begin{align*} \frac{dS}{dt}&=-\alpha SI\\ \frac{dI}{dt}&=\alpha SI-\beta I\\ \frac{dR}{dt}&=\beta I \end{align*}
    where \(\alpha\) is the “contagion” parameter and \(\beta\) is the “recovery” parameter. If we know \(S(t)\) and \(I (t)\text{,}\) then \(R(t) = 1-(S(t) + I (t))\text{.}\) Consequently, we need only keep track of \(S(t)\) and \(I (t)\text{,}\) and we can consider the planar (i.e 2D ) system
    \begin{align*} \frac{dS}{dt}&=&-\alpha SI\\ \frac{dI}{dt}&=&\alpha SI-\beta I \end{align*}
    The equilibria of this system are the solutions of the simultaneous system of equations
    \begin{align*} -\alpha SI&=&0\\ (\alpha S-\beta) I&=&0 \end{align*}
    which is precisely the line \(I = 0\text{.}\) This makes sense since if there are no infected, then no one can catch the disease. Notice that \(\frac{dI}{dt}=0\) if \(\alpha S-\beta=0\text{.}\) In other words if \(S=\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\text{,}\) the vectors in the vector field are horizontal. To the right of the vertical line in the \(SI-\) phase plane, \(\frac{dI}{dt}> 0\text{,}\) and the disease is spreading.To the left of the vertical line in the \(SI-\) phase plane, \(\frac{dI}{dt}< 0\text{,}\) and the disease is decreasing. Hence, the value \(S=\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\) plays an important role in the evolution of the disease. It is called the threshold value of the model. Given values of \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\text{,}\) if \(S(0) > \frac{\beta}{\alpha}\text{,}\) then an epidemic occurs. If \(S(0) < \frac{\beta}{\alpha}\text{,}\) then there is no epidemic.

    Section Hey, I got Covid-19 THREE times!

    1.

    One of the basic assumptions of the SIR model is that individuals who recover from the disease never get it again. However, diseases continually evolve, and new strains can emerge that can infect those who have recovered from the previous strain. In this project, we modify the SIR model so that recovered become susceptible again in a linear rate. We obtain the system of equations
    \begin{align*} \frac{dS}{dt}&-\alpha SI+\gamma R\\ \frac{dI}{dt}&=\alpha SI-\beta I\\ \frac{dR}{dt}&=\beta I-\gamma R \end{align*}
    🔗

    (a)

    Show that \(S(t)+I(t)+R(t)\) is a constant function of \(t\) for this model.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Derive a system in the two dependent variables \(S\) and \(I\) using the fact that \(R = 1 - (S + I )\) initially.
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    What are the equilibrium points for this model of the two variables \(S\) and \(I\text{?}\) (Hint: Both \(S\) and \(I(t)\) are nonnegative, and \(S(t) + I (t) \le 1\) for all \(t\text{.}\))
    🔗
    🔗

    Fix \(\alpha=0.3,\beta=0.15\) and \(\gamma=0.05\text{.}\) Assume that \(S(0)=0.99, I(0)=0.01\text{.}\) Create tables using Euler’s method for system with \(\Delta t=1\text{,}\) run your table down until you see something interesting!
